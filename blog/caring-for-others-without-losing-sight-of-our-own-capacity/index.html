Caregiving & Capacity

November 17, 2026

Caring for Others Without Losing Sight of Our Own Capacity

By Laura Camus, Ed.D., MA, MSW, CCMP Co-Founder & Executive Director, ThriveWell Care Collective™

Have you ever reached the end of a day and wondered how you managed to get everything done?

Maybe you've spent the day working, caring for a loved one, supporting colleagues, solving problems, or managing the responsibilities that seem to find their way onto your plate.

And even though you're exhausted, there's still something else that needs your attention.

I think many of us become so accustomed to being the person others depend on that we stop noticing how much we're carrying.

We tell ourselves, "It's just a busy week," or "Things will settle down soon."

Sometimes they do. But sometimes the demands continue, and we simply keep finding ways to manage them.

I've been thinking about what happens when we become so focused on meeting everyone else's needs that we lose sight of our own capacity.

Understanding Our Capacity

When I think about capacity, I'm not just thinking about how many tasks we can complete in a day.

I'm thinking about the physical, emotional, and mental energy we have available, along with the time, resources, and support we can draw upon to meet the responsibilities of our lives.

Some days, we have more energy and patience than others. We may feel rested, focused, and able to manage several competing priorities.

Other days, even ordinary responsibilities feel more difficult.

Maybe we haven't slept well. Maybe we're worried about someone we love. Perhaps work has been particularly demanding, or we're dealing with something difficult in our personal lives.

Our capacity isn't the same every day. And yet, we often expect ourselves to function as though it is.

I know I've fallen into that pattern myself.

When we're accustomed to being capable and dependable, it can be difficult to recognize that we may need to approach things differently.

Being capable doesn't mean having unlimited capacity.

That's an important distinction.

And it's one that deserves more attention, especially when caring for others is such a significant part of our lives.

The Demands of Caring for Others

Caregiving involves much more than the responsibilities we can easily see.

According to AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving (2025), approximately 63 million American adults provided ongoing care to adults or children with medical conditions or disabilities in the previous year. Many balance these responsibilities with employment, parenting, and other demands.

While those findings focus on family caregiving, the experience of managing competing responsibilities can also resonate with professional caregivers, educators, leaders, and others whose work involves supporting people.

In many of these roles, the responsibility extends beyond completing tasks.

It's remembering what needs to happen next, anticipating problems, responding to unexpected situations, making decisions, and being emotionally available when someone needs support.

And sometimes, it's trying to keep everything moving forward even when we're running low on energy ourselves.

The National Institute on Aging (n.d.) recognizes that caregiving can affect a person's physical and emotional well-being and emphasizes the importance of attending to caregivers' own health and support needs.

I think that matters because we sometimes talk about caregiving as though it is simply a matter of having enough time.

But time is only one part of the picture.

We also need energy, attention, patience, resources, and opportunities to recover.

When Caring Becomes Carrying

One thing I've noticed throughout my personal and professional life is how easily caring about others can turn into feeling responsible for everything.

We want to help. We want people to feel supported. We want to do a good job, meet expectations, and make a positive difference.

Those intentions are meaningful.

But sometimes we begin taking on responsibilities that don't entirely belong to us.

We anticipate problems before they happen. We step in before someone asks. We worry about how other people will respond, and we spend time trying to prevent disappointment or difficulty.

Before long, we're carrying not only our responsibilities but also our concerns about everyone else's experiences and decisions.

I've been learning that there's an important difference between caring about something and being responsible for its outcome.

We can offer support, share information, provide guidance, and fulfill our obligations without assuming responsibility for every choice another person makes.

Of course, some responsibilities genuinely belong to us.

For those who provide care professionally, patient safety, ethical standards, and the needs of people entrusted to our care require appropriate attention.

Family caregiving can involve equally significant responsibilities, particularly when someone relies on us for essential support.

Recognizing our capacity doesn't mean ignoring those obligations or withdrawing care from someone who needs it.

It means becoming more aware of what we're carrying, what genuinely requires our attention, and where additional support or a different approach might be needed.

Recognizing When We Need Support

Sometimes the signs that we're stretched too thin are easy to miss.

We may notice that we're becoming impatient more quickly than usual. We might have difficulty concentrating, feel constantly rushed, or find that activities we normally enjoy no longer seem appealing.

Perhaps we feel guilty when we rest because there's always something else we could be doing.

These experiences can have many causes. They aren't necessarily signs of caregiver fatigue, but they may be opportunities to check in with ourselves.

Instead of immediately asking, "How can I get everything done?" perhaps we could ask:

What do I need in order to manage what is being asked of me?

That's a different question.

It invites us to think about resources, support, realistic expectations, and our own well-being.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024) encourages family caregivers to arrange regular breaks and seek help from family, friends, and community resources.

Sometimes support looks like asking someone to help with an appointment or share a household responsibility.

Other times, it means having an honest conversation about what we're managing and what additional resources might be needed.

And sometimes, simply acknowledging that we're stretched too thin is an important place to begin.

Making Room for Our Own Needs

I don't believe supporting our capacity requires us to make dramatic changes overnight.

Sometimes it begins with noticing where our energy is going and considering what could be different.

Here are a few possibilities worth exploring.

Recognize what genuinely requires your attention. Not every request is equally urgent, and not every problem is ours to solve. Consider which responsibilities are essential, which could be shared, and which expectations we've placed on ourselves.Ask for or accept support. Whether it's sharing a household responsibility, talking with someone we trust, or asking a colleague for assistance, support can take many forms.Build small restorative moments into the day. A brief walk, a few minutes of quiet, gentle movement, or a guided relaxation practice may provide a welcome opportunity to pause and reconnect with ourselves.Communicate when expectations are unrealistic. Sometimes the issue isn't how we're managing our time. It's that we're being asked to do more than available time and resources allow.Give ourselves permission to recognize our limits. Having a limit doesn't mean we're less caring, committed, or capable.

Of course, not everyone has the same freedom to change their circumstances.

Some caregivers have limited access to respite, financial resources, or additional help. Professionals may work in environments where staffing shortages, excessive workloads, or competing demands cannot be resolved through individual wellness practices.

The Office of the U.S. Surgeon General (2022) identifies adequate rest, supportive relationships, worker input, and work-life harmony as important components of workplace well-being.

That's an important reminder that well-being is influenced by both our individual choices and the circumstances in which we live and work.

We may not have the authority to change every circumstance, but we can recognize what is affecting us, communicate our needs where possible, and explore available sources of support.

And organizations have a responsibility to listen to employees and consider the conditions that influence their well-being.

A Moment for Reflection

If you have a journal nearby, consider spending a few minutes with these questions:

  1. What responsibilities are taking the most energy from me right now?

  2. When during the day do I notice that my energy or patience is running low?

  3. Where might I be taking on responsibilities that could be shared?

  4. Am I spending energy worrying about situations or decisions beyond my control?

  5. What kind of support would make the greatest difference in my life right now?

  6. What is one small thing I could do this week to recognize and respect my own capacity?

You might also complete this sentence:

I've been giving a lot of my energy to ______, and I would like to make a little more room for ______.

There's no need to solve everything today.

Sometimes recognizing what we're experiencing is the beginning of making a meaningful change.

One Small Step Toward Greater Awareness

As you move through the coming week, consider choosing one responsibility or expectation to examine a little more closely.

Ask yourself whether it genuinely requires your attention, whether someone else could share it, or whether you've been holding yourself to an expectation that no longer makes sense.

Perhaps you'll decide to ask for help.

Maybe you'll have a conversation about what you need.

Or perhaps you'll simply recognize that you've been carrying more than you realized.

Whatever you discover, try approaching it with curiosity rather than criticism.

The goal isn't to become less caring or less dependable.

It's to become more aware of how we use our energy and how we can care for ourselves alongside the people who depend on us.

Caring for Ourselves Is Part of Caring for Others

At ThriveWell Care Collective™, we believe the people who care for, support, teach, serve, and lead others deserve opportunities to receive care themselves.

Through our Arrive • Notice • Release • Return™ approach, we encourage people to pause, recognize what they're experiencing, make room for restoration, and reconnect with what matters.

For me, that includes learning to recognize that my energy and attention are valuable resources.

I can care deeply about others without expecting myself to have unlimited time, patience, or energy.

I can be dependable while also acknowledging when I need help.

And I can recognize that my own well-being deserves consideration alongside the needs of those I support.

A final thought to consider:

What might change if I began treating my own capacity with the same care and attention I give to everyone else's needs?

It's a question worth returning to.
