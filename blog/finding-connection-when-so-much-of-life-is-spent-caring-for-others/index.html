November 17, 2026

Finding Connection When So Much of Life Is Spent Caring for Others

By Laura Camus, Ed.D., MA, MSW, CCMP Co-Founder & Executive Director, ThriveWell Care Collective™

I've been thinking lately about the difference between being surrounded by people and feeling connected to them.

For many of us, our days are filled with interactions.

We talk with family members, coworkers, clients, patients, students, and people in our communities. We answer questions, listen to concerns, offer support, and help others work through challenges.

Sometimes we spend nearly the entire day attending to other people.

And yet, it's still possible to reach the end of the day feeling a little disconnected.

I think that's something worth talking about, especially for those of us who spend so much of our time caring for and supporting others.

What Does It Mean to Feel Connected?

Meaningful connection involves more than simply having people around us. It's the experience of feeling heard, valued, supported, and accepted in our relationships.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024a) distinguishes between loneliness and social isolation.

Loneliness is the feeling of being alone or disconnected from others, particularly when our relationships don't provide the closeness or sense of belonging we desire.Social isolation refers to having limited relationships, social contact, or support from others.

The distinction matters because we can experience loneliness even when we're regularly surrounded by people.

We might spend an entire day communicating with coworkers, assisting family members, or responding to others' needs without a single conversation in which we feel genuinely heard.

And someone who enjoys spending time alone may still have meaningful relationships and a strong sense of connection.

I've also been thinking about the importance of belonging.

We may know many people, but do we feel welcome, included, and comfortable being ourselves?

For me, meaningful community isn't simply about bringing people together. It's about creating opportunities for people to feel that they have a place, that their presence matters, and that they don't always have to be the person taking care of everyone else.

The important question isn't necessarily how many people we interact with.

It's whether we have relationships where we feel comfortable being ourselves and receiving support when we need it.

When We're Always the One Listening

Throughout my career in social work, education, leadership, and organizational change, I've had many opportunities to listen to people share their experiences, challenges, concerns, and hopes.

I've always valued those conversations.

There's something meaningful about helping someone feel heard and understood.

But I've also been learning that relationships feel different when we're always the person listening, supporting, or helping.

Sometimes we become so accustomed to asking how everyone else is doing that we forget to consider whether anyone knows how we're doing.

We may have plenty of conversations throughout the day, but very few where we feel comfortable talking about our own experiences.

And when life gets busy, those deeper connections can become harder to maintain.

I've also noticed that being someone others depend on can gradually become part of how we see ourselves.

We're the dependable friend, the supportive colleague, the person who figures things out, or the family member who keeps everything moving forward.

Those can be meaningful roles.

But they're not the only parts of who we are.

We need opportunities to be more than the person who is always available to help.

Connection Is Also About Letting Ourselves Be Known

One thing I've been learning is that meaningful connection isn't only about what we give to others.

It's also about allowing others to know and support us.

That isn't always easy.

When someone asks how we're doing, our automatic response may be, "I'm fine," even when we're managing something difficult.

Sometimes we don't want to burden someone else. Sometimes we're unsure how much to share. And sometimes we've become so accustomed to being the person who helps that receiving attention feels unfamiliar.

Of course, we don't need to share personal details with everyone.

Trust develops over time, and it's important to recognize which relationships feel safe, respectful, and supportive.

But when we have people we trust, perhaps we can allow ourselves to be a little more open.

Maybe we tell a friend we've had a difficult week.

Maybe we acknowledge that we're worried about something.

Or perhaps we simply accept an invitation to spend time together without feeling that we need to accomplish anything.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024b) encourages people to develop relationships that involve both giving and receiving support.

I think that's an important reminder.

We can be there for others while allowing others to be there for us, too.

Why Connection Matters for Caregivers

Caregiving can be deeply meaningful, but it can also change how we spend our time and energy.

Family caregivers may find that arranging social opportunities becomes more difficult because someone depends on them at home.

Professional caregivers may spend their workdays surrounded by people, yet have little opportunity to connect with others outside the responsibilities of their roles.

Leaders, educators, and people who regularly support others may feel pressure to remain composed, positive, or available, even when they're managing their own challenges.

And some caregivers simply feel that they don't have the time or energy to maintain relationships.

These experiences aren't uncommon.

According to AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving (2025), approximately 24% of surveyed family caregivers caring for adults reported feeling alone, compared with 21% in 2020.

That finding is especially meaningful because caregiving often involves ongoing interaction with another person.

Being needed by others doesn't necessarily mean we feel supported ourselves.

Research also tells us that meaningful social relationships are an important part of overall well-being.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024a) identifies loneliness and social isolation as factors associated with increased risks of physical and mental health conditions, including depression, anxiety, heart disease, stroke, and dementia.

These findings don't mean that everyone who experiences loneliness will develop a health condition. They do reinforce that meaningful connection deserves attention alongside other aspects of our well-being.

For caregivers, that may mean recognizing that relationships outside our caregiving responsibilities are important, too.

We don't always have to navigate everything by ourselves.

Connection Matters in the Workplace, Too

I also think it's important to recognize the role connection plays in our professional lives.

When we spend much of our day caring for patients, supporting clients, teaching students, or leading colleagues, our workplace relationships can influence how supported and included we feel.

There's a difference between working alongside people and feeling that we're part of a supportive team.

Having colleagues who listen, leaders who invite honest conversations, and opportunities to build respectful relationships can help create a sense of belonging.

Sometimes meaningful workplace connection happens when a colleague takes a few minutes to check in, offers assistance during a demanding day, or listens without immediately trying to solve a problem.

Of course, supportive relationships don't eliminate excessive workloads, staffing challenges, or organizational pressures.

Those concerns require appropriate attention, resources, and organizational responses.

But relationships still matter.

When people feel comfortable communicating, asking questions, sharing concerns, and offering support, there may be more opportunities to develop trust and understanding.

And for those of us in leadership roles, that raises an important question:

Are we creating opportunities for people to feel heard and included, or are we so focused on completing the work that we overlook the relationships supporting it?

It's something worth considering.

Connection Doesn't Have to Be Complicated

When we think about community, we sometimes imagine large gatherings, organized activities, or groups of people who share a common interest.

Those experiences can be wonderful.

But I think meaningful connection can happen in much smaller ways.

It might be a conversation with a friend who really listens.

A coworker who notices we've had a difficult day.

A neighbor who checks in.

Someone who remembers something important to us and takes a moment to ask about it.

Or a group where we feel comfortable showing up without having to explain or justify everything we're experiencing.

I don't think connection is measured by how many people we know or how often we're surrounded by others.

Sometimes one meaningful relationship provides more comfort than dozens of casual interactions.

And connection doesn't always require talking about something difficult.

It can happen through shared laughter, walking together, participating in an activity, or simply enjoying someone's company.

Making Room for Genuine Connection

Something I've been learning is that meaningful connection doesn't have to become another responsibility.

We don't need to add more commitments to an already full calendar simply because we think we should be more social.

Instead, perhaps we can begin by noticing the relationships and opportunities already available to us.

Here are a few possibilities worth considering.

Reach out without waiting for a special occasion. A simple message saying, "I've been thinking about you. How are you doing?" can open the door to a meaningful conversation.Make space for relationships that feel supportive. Consider the people with whom you feel comfortable being yourself rather than feeling that you always need to help or fulfill a particular role.Allow yourself to receive attention and care. When someone asks how you're doing, consider whether you'd like to share a little more than your usual "I'm fine."Explore shared experiences. A community activity, book group, walking group, wellness workshop, or reflection circle may provide opportunities to meet people with similar interests.Reconnect through everyday activities. A walk with a friend, a conversation over coffee, or a phone call while you're preparing dinner may feel more manageable than adding another scheduled commitment.Recognize that connection can look different for everyone. Some people enjoy groups and conversation. Others prefer quiet time with one trusted person. Neither approach is better.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024b) recommends small, manageable actions such as checking in with people we care about, participating in shared activities, and nurturing supportive relationships.

And sometimes connection begins by simply being present and listening without trying to fix anything.

That can be meaningful for both people.

When Connection Feels Difficult

I also think it's important to acknowledge that building and maintaining relationships isn't always easy.

Some people have experienced disappointment, conflict, or loss.

Others may be caring for someone whose needs make it difficult to leave home or participate in community activities.

Some may have limited transportation, financial resources, or access to programs that fit their schedules.

And others may be navigating life transitions that have changed their social circles.

It isn't always as simple as deciding to get out more.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024a) recognizes that factors such as access to transportation, community resources, and opportunities for participation can influence social connection.

That's an important reminder that connection isn't entirely an individual responsibility.

Communities and organizations also influence whether people have opportunities to build relationships and feel included.

Accessible activities, supportive workplace relationships, flexible opportunities for participation, and welcoming community spaces can help make connection more possible.

For someone who cannot easily attend an in-person gathering, a phone conversation or virtual group might offer a meaningful alternative.

For someone who feels overwhelmed by social expectations, spending time with one trusted person may be enough.

And for someone who isn't ready to connect with others right now, taking time to reflect on their needs can be a worthwhile first step.

If feelings of loneliness or disconnection persist or begin affecting your emotional well-being or daily life, consider speaking with a healthcare or mental health professional. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024b) also recommends seeking professional support when feelings of loneliness, disconnection, or stress become difficult to manage.

Connection should feel supportive, not like another obligation.

A Moment for Reflection

If you enjoy journaling, consider spending a few minutes with these questions:

  1. When do I feel most connected to other people?

  2. Are there relationships in my life where I feel comfortable sharing my own needs and experiences?

  3. Do I sometimes spend so much time supporting others that I overlook my own need for connection?

  4. Is there someone I've been meaning to reach out to?

  5. What makes a relationship feel supportive, respectful, and meaningful to me?

  6. What kind of community or connection would feel manageable for me right now?

You might also complete this sentence:

I feel most supported and connected when ______.

Take a moment to think about your answer.

What does it tell you about the kinds of relationships and experiences that matter most to you?

One Small Step Toward Connection

As you move through the coming week, consider one small opportunity to connect with someone.

Perhaps there's a friend you've been meaning to call, a colleague you'd enjoy spending a few minutes with, or a community activity you've been considering.

You might send a simple message:

"I've been thinking about you and would love to catch up sometime."

It doesn't need to become a lengthy conversation or another commitment on your calendar.

Maybe you're ready to share a little more honestly when someone asks how you're doing.

Or perhaps you'd simply like to spend some uninterrupted time with someone whose company you enjoy.

And if you're the person who usually listens, perhaps you can allow yourself to share something about your own experience, too.

Connection doesn't have to begin with a major commitment.

Sometimes it begins with one meaningful conversation.

And if connecting with others feels difficult right now, that's worth acknowledging, too.

You can approach it at a pace that feels comfortable.

The goal isn't to build a larger social circle or fill every available moment with activity.

It's to recognize what meaningful connection looks like for you and consider how to make a little more room for it.

Creating Spaces Where People Feel Welcome

One of the things I value about community is the opportunity it creates for people to come together without needing to have everything figured out.

We don't always need advice.

We don't always need someone to solve our problems.

Sometimes we simply want to know that someone understands, that our experiences matter, and that we're welcome to be ourselves.

At ThriveWell Care Collective™, we want to create opportunities for people who care for others to experience that kind of connection and belonging.

Through guided reflection, educational wellness experiences, virtual programs, community gatherings, and opportunities for shared conversation, we hope to create welcoming spaces where people can pause, connect, and consider their own well-being.

Our goal isn't to add another responsibility to anyone's life.

It's to offer opportunities for people to reconnect with themselves and with others in ways that feel meaningful to them.

We also recognize that community experiences aren't a replacement for professional care or other support someone may need. They are opportunities for reflection, shared experiences, and connection that can complement other forms of support.

A Final Thought

I've been reminded that we can spend our lives being there for other people while forgetting that we need people in our lives who are there for us, too.

That doesn't mean every relationship needs to be perfectly balanced or that every conversation needs to be deeply personal.

It simply means recognizing that our own need for connection deserves attention.

Who helps you feel heard, supported, and accepted just as you are?

And if someone comes to mind, perhaps this is a good week to reach out.
