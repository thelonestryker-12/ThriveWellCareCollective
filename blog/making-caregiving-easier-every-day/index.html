Making Caregiving Easier Every Day

By Laura Camus, Ed.D., MA, MSW, CCMP With contributions from Toni Shankweiler, OTR, retired

When you care for someone else, even ordinary tasks can begin to take more energy than you realize.

Helping someone get dressed. Preparing meals. Managing medications. Getting in and out of the car. Keeping track of appointments. Doing laundry. Cleaning. Working. Parenting. Answering the phone one more time.

None of these tasks may seem overwhelming on their own. But together, they can create a steady physical, mental, and emotional drain.

Caregivers often become so focused on meeting someone else’s needs that they begin overlooking their own. They may feel responsible for doing everything, hesitate to ask for help, or believe that taking time for themselves means neglecting someone they love.

Yet caring for yourself is an important part of sustaining your ability to care for others.

One helpful shift is to stop asking only, “How do I get everything done?” and begin asking, “How can I make what I need to do easier on my body, my mind, and my energy?”

That question is central to ThriveWell Care Collective™ and our commitment to restoring energy, empathy, and capacity for those who care.

1. Notice Where Your Energy Is Going

Caregivers often move from one responsibility to another without recognizing how much physical and emotional effort their routines require.

Begin by observing rather than judging.

  • Which activities leave you especially tired?
  • Which parts of the day feel rushed?
  • Which tasks require repeated bending, lifting, or carrying?
  • Which responsibilities take more mental or emotional energy than expected?
  • When do you feel most overwhelmed or depleted?

Noticing these patterns can help you identify where adjustments may be useful.

A few minutes of mindful awareness, grounding, or guided breathing can also provide an opportunity to check in with yourself before moving to the next responsibility.

You do not have to change everything at once. Sometimes the first step is simply noticing.

2. Plan Ahead and Conserve Your Energy

Occupational therapy emphasizes helping people participate in meaningful daily activities through approaches that may include planning, prioritizing, adapting routines, and modifying environments (American Occupational Therapy Association [AOTA], n.d.).

Occupational therapy research on fatigue management identifies approaches such as activity pacing, balancing activity and rest, setting priorities, and adapting tasks or environments. These studies primarily examined people living with chronic health conditions rather than caregivers; results for fatigue reduction varied across interventions (Kim et al., 2022; Omura et al., 2022). The approaches below are practical examples, not evidence of proven caregiver-specific outcomes.

In everyday caregiving, these approaches might include:

  • Gathering supplies before beginning an activity rather than making several trips.
  • Keeping frequently used items within easy reach.
  • Preparing meals or portions of meals in advance.
  • Grouping errands and appointments when practical.
  • Breaking larger tasks into smaller, manageable steps.
  • Alternating physically demanding activities with lighter ones.
  • Allowing additional time for activities that often feel rushed.

Planning is not about squeezing more responsibilities into the day. It is about reducing unnecessary effort.

The same principle applies to your own well-being. Rather than waiting until you are completely exhausted, consider scheduling time for rest, social connection, relaxation, or another activity that helps you recharge.

3. Recognize What You Can Do and Where You Need Help

One of the most important caregiving skills is recognizing the difference between what you can reasonably manage yourself and what requires additional support.

Many caregivers believe they should be able to handle everything independently. Some worry that asking for help will make them appear incapable. Others do not want to burden family members or friends.

Trust can also be a concern. When you know a loved one’s needs and routines intimately, it may be difficult to feel comfortable allowing someone else to provide care.

These concerns are understandable, but trying to do everything yourself can become physically and emotionally exhausting.

Consider asking:

  • What responsibilities can I manage safely and realistically?
  • Which activities require skills, equipment, or assistance I may not have?
  • What could someone else do to make my day easier?
  • Are there tasks I continue doing myself because I feel guilty asking for help?
  • What information or preparation would help me feel more comfortable accepting assistance?

Help does not always mean asking someone to take over caregiving completely.

It might mean having a family member prepare dinner, asking a friend to pick up groceries, arranging transportation to an appointment, or having someone stay with your loved one while you take a break (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [CDC], 2024).

For hands-on care, especially lifting, transferring, or assisting someone with mobility, it may mean obtaining guidance or assistance from an appropriately qualified healthcare professional.

Asking for help is not a measure of your ability to care. It is a way of recognizing that caregiving should not require you to sacrifice your own well-being.

4. Protect Your Body During Daily Caregiving

Caregiving can place considerable demands on your body, particularly when helping someone with bathing, dressing, positioning, transfers, or mobility.

Repeated bending, reaching, lifting, and working in awkward positions may contribute to physical discomfort or injury.

Pay attention to what your body is telling you.

Pain, persistent discomfort, or increasing physical difficulty should not simply be accepted as an unavoidable part of caregiving.

Consider these practical precautions:

  • Avoid lifting or transferring someone when you cannot do so safely.
  • Use recommended assistive equipment and obtain training in its proper use.
  • Be mindful of your posture and body mechanics.
  • Reduce unnecessary bending, twisting, or reaching.
  • Ask for assistance when an activity exceeds your physical abilities.
  • Seek medical attention when pain or other symptoms warrant evaluation.

An occupational therapist, physical therapist, nurse, or another qualified healthcare professional may be able to provide individualized recommendations for safer caregiving activities (AOTA, n.d.).

Protecting your body also means making room for movement and relaxation.

Taking a walk, engaging in appropriate exercise, enjoying a warm bath, or scheduling a massage can provide time to focus on your own physical comfort and well-being.

At ThriveWell, our licensed massage therapist provides mobile massage services designed to create an opportunity for relaxation and restorative self-care.

Massage is not a substitute for medical treatment, physical therapy, or occupational therapy. It can, however, provide dedicated time when the caregiver becomes the person receiving care. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH, 2019) discusses massage as a complementary approach and notes that evidence for particular benefits varies by condition and outcome.

5. Recognize and Express Your Feelings

Caregiving can involve a wide range of emotions.

You may feel grateful for the opportunity to care for someone you love while also feeling sad, lonely, frustrated, guilty, or angry.

Sometimes these feelings occur together.

You might feel frustrated by the demands of caregiving and then guilty for feeling frustrated. You may miss the freedom you once had or feel isolated from people who do not understand your daily responsibilities.

These emotional responses can be a normal part of a demanding caregiving experience.

Rather than ignoring or judging your feelings, consider finding someone you trust who can listen.

That person might be a family member, friend, support-group participant, clergy member, therapist, counselor, or another professional (CDC, 2024).

Support groups can be especially valuable because they offer opportunities to connect with people who understand similar experiences.

You do not have to wait until you feel overwhelmed to seek emotional support.

You can care deeply about someone and still acknowledge that caregiving is difficult. Both experiences can be true.

6. Stay Connected to Other People

Caregiving can gradually become isolating.

Appointments, household responsibilities, and the needs of a loved one may leave little time for friendships, hobbies, or social activities.

Over time, you may find yourself declining invitations, missing gatherings, or feeling disconnected from activities you once enjoyed.

Social connection deserves a place in your routine.

Consider arranging a phone call with a friend, meeting someone for coffee, attending a community activity, or spending time doing something you enjoy.

If leaving home is difficult, look for opportunities to connect virtually or invite someone to visit.

When appropriate and feasible, ask a trusted friend or family member to stay with your loved one while you take time for yourself. Respite-care services or qualified paid caregivers may also provide options, depending on your loved one’s needs and available resources (CDC, 2024).

Even a brief change of scenery or conversation unrelated to caregiving can be meaningful.

Giving yourself permission to socialize does not diminish your commitment to the person you care for.

Your relationships, interests, and sense of identity matter too.

7. Make Your Physical and Mental Health a Priority

Caregivers frequently schedule appointments, prepare meals, manage medications, and monitor the health of others while postponing their own needs.

Your health deserves the same consideration.

Try to maintain regular healthcare appointments and recommended screenings. Pay attention to nutrition, hydration, sleep, and physical activity appropriate for your health and abilities (CDC, 2024).

Mental well-being also requires attention.

Activities such as reading, listening to favorite music, practicing meditation, participating in yoga, or spending quiet time outdoors can create opportunities to relax and reconnect with yourself.

You may find that different activities support you on different days.

Some days, a walk may feel restorative. Other days, you may need rest, conversation, music, or a few minutes of quiet.

At ThriveWell, our nonclinical wellness experiences include guided breathing, mindfulness, grounding, and guided relaxation. These practices offer opportunities to pause, direct attention to the present moment, and make space for your own well-being.

Research reviewed by NCCIH (2022) suggests that some relaxation and mindfulness approaches may help with stress, although outcomes vary.

These practices complement, rather than replace, appropriate medical or mental-health care.

The goal is not to create another list of things you feel obligated to accomplish.

It is to identify what genuinely supports your health and make room for it when possible.

8. Give Yourself Permission to Pause

Many caregivers rest only when they have reached the point of exhaustion.

Yet rest does not always require an entire afternoon or a day away from responsibilities.

Sometimes it looks like sitting for a few minutes between activities.

Sometimes it means choosing a simpler meal, leaving a household task until tomorrow, or asking someone else to make a phone call.

Sometimes it is taking a few slow, comfortable breaths before responding to the next request.

And sometimes it means arranging a longer break so that you can participate in an activity that restores your energy.

The CDC (2024) encourages caregivers to attend to their own health and seek breaks and support when possible.

Rather than treating rest as something you earn after everything else is completed, consider how it might become a regular part of your routine.

Rest is not a reward for finishing your responsibilities. It is one of the ways you support your ability to continue.

9. Create an Environment That Supports You

Sometimes making caregiving easier involves changing how your surroundings are organized.

Consider whether frequently used supplies could be moved closer to where they are needed.

Could part of a task be completed safely while seated?

Could you reduce repeated trips between rooms?

Would an appropriate assistive device make an activity safer or more manageable?

Small environmental adjustments can reduce unnecessary physical effort.

Occupational therapy may include recommendations related to adaptive equipment, daily routines, caregiver training, and home safety and accessibility (AOTA, n.d.).

The same general principle can apply to your own restorative routines.

Create a comfortable place to sit, read, reflect, or practice relaxation.

Keep a favorite book nearby. Make space for music. Find a quiet corner where you can pause without feeling that you must immediately move on to another task.

Your environment does not need to be perfect to support your well-being.

Sometimes a small change is enough to make a difficult day a little more manageable.

10. Remember That You Matter, Too

There is no perfect caregiving routine.

Some days will be more demanding than others. Responsibilities may change, and the support you need today may be different from what you need next month.

What matters is recognizing that your needs remain important even when someone else depends on you.

You deserve opportunities to rest, connect with others, maintain your health, express your feelings, and receive support.

You do not have to do everything yourself.

You do not have to wait until you are exhausted to ask for help.

And you do not have to justify making time for something that brings you comfort, enjoyment, or peace.

Caregiving may be an important part of your life, but it is not the entirety of who you are.

A Moment for Reflection

Before moving on with your day, consider these questions:

  • What part of my caregiving routine requires the most physical or emotional energy?
  • What is one responsibility I could simplify, delegate, or ask for help with?
  • Have I been ignoring any signs that my body or mind needs attention?
  • When was the last time I spent time with someone simply because I enjoyed their company?
  • What is one realistic thing I can do for myself this week?

You might write your responses in a journal, discuss them with someone you trust, or simply spend a few quiet minutes reflecting.

You do not need to have all the answers.

Choose one small step that feels manageable.
