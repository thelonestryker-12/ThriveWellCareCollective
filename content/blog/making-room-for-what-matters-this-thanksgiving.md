Thanksgiving is just a few days away, and for many of us, that means there's a lot happening.

There are meals to plan, groceries to buy, tables to set, family schedules to coordinate, and traditions to keep alive.

And somewhere in the middle of all that preparation, we might find ourselves worrying about whether everything will turn out the way we hope.

Will everyone get along? Will the food be ready on time? Will everyone feel included? Will the day be what we imagined?

I've been thinking about how much energy we sometimes spend trying to create the perfect holiday.

Especially when we're the ones everyone depends on.

## When Expectations Take Over

I think most of us want the holidays to be meaningful.

We want people to enjoy themselves, feel welcome, and leave with good memories. There's something wonderful about bringing people together and creating those experiences.

But sometimes, without realizing it, we take on responsibility for more than the meal, the decorations, or the gathering itself.

We start feeling responsible for everyone's happiness.

We worry about whether someone will be disappointed, whether a conversation might become uncomfortable, or whether something will happen that changes the day's mood.

And before we know it, we're trying to manage things we really can't control.

I've been learning that I can prepare thoughtfully, welcome people warmly, and do my part to create a meaningful experience.

But I can't determine how everyone else will feel, behave, or respond.

That's something I've had to remind myself more than once.

## What Can I Actually Control?

One of the most helpful questions I've learned to ask is:

*What part of this is actually mine?*

I can decide how much I'm willing to take on.

I can ask for help instead of trying to do everything myself.

I can communicate kindly, set reasonable boundaries, and decide how I want to respond when something doesn't go as planned.

But I can't control another person's mood, choices, or expectations.

And I can't make every moment of a holiday perfect.

Recognizing those limits doesn't mean I care any less.

It means I can put more energy into the things I can influence and less into trying to manage what I cannot.

## Gratitude Doesn't Mean Everything Is Wonderful

Thanksgiving is a time when we're encouraged to think about gratitude.

I appreciate that tradition. But I've also come to understand that gratitude doesn't require us to pretend everything is wonderful.

Sometimes the holidays are complicated.

We may be missing someone, worried about a family member, managing difficult relationships, or simply feeling tired from everything life has asked of us.

We can be grateful for the people and experiences that bring meaning to our lives while acknowledging that some things are difficult.

Both can be true.

Maybe gratitude this year is appreciating the people who show up, a meaningful conversation, someone offering to help with the dishes, or a few quiet minutes after everyone has gone home.

It doesn't have to be anything extraordinary.

## A Thanksgiving Journaling Moment

Before the holiday arrives, consider spending a few minutes reflecting on what matters most to you.

**Journaling Prompts**

1. What expectations am I placing on myself for Thanksgiving? Are they realistic?
2. Am I taking responsibility for how other people might feel or behave?
3. What can I do to make the holiday meaningful without exhausting myself?
4. Is there something I could ask for help with or give myself permission to do differently?
5. What am I genuinely grateful for this year, even if some parts of life feel difficult?

You might also try finishing this sentence:

*This Thanksgiving, I want to spend less energy on \_\_\_\_\_\_ and more energy on \_\_\_\_\_\_.*

See what comes to mind.

## Giving Yourself Permission to Enjoy the Day

This year, perhaps we can approach Thanksgiving with a little less pressure.

The food doesn't have to be perfect. Not every tradition has to happen exactly as it always has. And we don't have to take responsibility for everyone else's experience.

We can care, prepare, welcome, and contribute without trying to control every outcome.

And when something doesn't go as planned, we can remind ourselves that one imperfect moment doesn't have to define the entire day.

At ThriveWell, we believe restoration often begins with recognizing what we need and making room for it.

Maybe this Thanksgiving, that means giving yourself permission to sit down and enjoy the meal you've helped create, accepting someone's offer to help, or letting a few things be good enough.

## A Thought for Thanksgiving

**What might I enjoy more fully if I stopped trying to make everything turn out exactly as I imagined?**

However you're spending Thanksgiving this year, I hope you find a few moments of connection, gratitude, and rest.

And if you're the person who usually takes care of everyone else, I hope you remember that you're allowed to enjoy the day, too.
