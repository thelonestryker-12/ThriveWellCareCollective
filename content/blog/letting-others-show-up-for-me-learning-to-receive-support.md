I've spent a good part of my life being someone others can depend on.

Whether at home, at work, or in my community, I've often been comfortable stepping in, offering help, or figuring out what needs to happen next.

But I've been thinking about something lately.

**Why is it sometimes easier to offer help than to accept it?**

When someone asks if they can do something for us, how often do we respond with, "I'm good," "Don't worry about it," or "I've got it"?

Sometimes we really do have things under control. Other times, I think we're so accustomed to being the person who helps that we forget we're allowed to need support ourselves.

## When Being Dependable Becomes a Habit

There's something meaningful about being someone others trust.

I value being dependable. I enjoy helping people work through challenges, and much of my professional life has involved supporting others through change.

But I've also begun to recognize that being capable doesn't mean I have unlimited capacity.

And knowing how to manage something doesn't mean I always have to manage it alone.

Sometimes we assume that accepting help means we're inconveniencing someone else. Sometimes we're concerned that things won't be done the way we would do them. And sometimes we simply don't know how to ask.

Those are patterns worth noticing.

## Making Room for Someone Else

One thing I'm learning is that support doesn't always have to look the way I expect.

Someone might offer to take care of a task, listen without judgment, prepare a meal, help with an appointment, or simply spend time with me.

It may not be exactly what I would have planned, but that doesn't make it less meaningful.

I'm also discovering that letting someone help isn't the same as giving up responsibility.

It's recognizing that relationships and communities are not meant to be one-sided.

For those of us who care for others professionally, this can be particularly difficult. We're accustomed to noticing what others need, often before they ask. But we have needs, too.

Sometimes support means asking a colleague for assistance, communicating that our workload is too much, or acknowledging that we could use a break.

And sometimes it means allowing someone who genuinely wants to help us to do just that.

## A Few Questions for Your Journal

As December begins and responsibilities start piling up, I invite you to consider:

1.  When someone offers to help me, how do I usually respond?

2.  What makes it difficult for me to ask for or accept support?

3.  Am I holding on to something because I truly need to manage it, or because I'm uncomfortable letting someone else handle it?

4.  Who in my life has shown that I can count on them?

5.  What is one small way I could allow myself to receive care this week?

You don't need to answer every question. Choose the one that feels most relevant.

## A Small Change for the Week Ahead

As the holiday season gets busier, perhaps this is a good time to practice receiving rather than always providing.

If someone offers to help, maybe we can consider saying yes.

If we need something, perhaps we can practice asking.

And if the support we need isn't available right now, we can still acknowledge that the need is real and consider other resources or possibilities.

At ThriveWell Care Collective™, our Arrive • Notice • Release • Return™ approach reminds us to slow down long enough to recognize what we're experiencing and what might help.

For me, that includes remembering that I don't have to be the one holding everything together all the time.

**One thought to carry into your week:**

*Where might I allow someone to help me this week?*

Maybe receiving care is another way of learning to care for ourselves.
