Have you ever found yourself trying to solve a problem that wasn't really yours to solve?

Maybe someone you care about is making a decision you disagree with. Maybe a coworker isn't following through on something. Or perhaps you're watching a situation unfold and thinking, *If everyone would just listen to me, this could be so much easier.*

I can relate.

In my professional life, I've spent years helping organizations and individuals work through change. Identifying challenges, developing solutions, and helping people move forward are things I've been trained to do.

And those skills have served me well.

But I've also learned that skills that are valuable in one part of our lives don't always translate neatly into another.

Especially when it comes to relationships.

## Not Every Problem Needs My Solution

When you care about someone, it's natural to want to help.

Whether we're caring for a family member, supporting someone through a difficult time, or leading a team at work, it can be easy to feel responsible for finding the solution. But sometimes, the most meaningful support we can offer is simply being present, listening, and allowing others to make their own choices.

You see something that concerns you, and you start thinking about what could make it better.

Maybe you offer advice. Maybe you do a little research. Maybe you think through different scenarios and try to anticipate what could go wrong.

And sometimes, that's genuinely helpful.

But other times, the person isn't looking for a solution. Or they're not ready to make a change. Or the situation simply isn't something you can change.

That's where things can become frustrating.

I've noticed that when I become too focused on what someone else should do, I can lose sight of something important: what I need and what choices are available to me.

## Recognizing the Limits of My Influence

One thing I've learned through my work in organizational change is that we can create conditions that support change, but we cannot make someone else embrace it.

We can provide information, encouragement, resources, and opportunities.

What another person chooses to do with those things is ultimately up to them.

I've been learning to apply that same understanding in my personal life.

I can express a concern without insisting that someone agrees with me.

I can offer support without taking over.

I can establish boundaries without expecting someone else to like them.

And I can recognize when continuing to push for a particular outcome is taking more from me than it's accomplishing.

None of that means becoming indifferent or ignoring situations that require action, particularly when safety is involved.

It means learning to distinguish between taking appropriate action and trying to control another person's choices.

## When My Own Well-Being Gets Lost

Something else I've been reflecting on is how easily our attention can become consumed by what other people are doing.

We replay conversations.

We worry about what might happen next.

We think about what we should have said or what we could have done differently to help someone understand.

And sometimes, without realizing it, our own peace of mind becomes dependent on whether someone else changes.

I've begun asking myself:

*Am I spending so much energy thinking about someone else's choices that I'm neglecting my own needs?*

That's not always a comfortable question.

But it helps me remember that while I may not be able to change a situation, I can often choose how I respond, what I participate in, and how I care for myself along the way.

## A Few Questions for Your Journal

Now that Thanksgiving has passed and we're moving toward another busy holiday season, this might be a good time to reflect on where our energy has been going.

**Journaling Prompts**

1. Is there a situation I've been trying to fix that isn't entirely within my control?
2. How much time and energy am I spending thinking about what someone else should do?
3. What have I already tried, and what have those efforts taught me?
4. What choices or boundaries are available to me, regardless of what someone else decides?
5. If I stopped trying to solve this situation for someone else, what could I do with that time and energy instead?

You may find yourself returning to one of these questions more than once.

That's okay.

Reflection isn't always about reaching an immediate conclusion. Sometimes it's about gradually understanding ourselves a little better.

## Making Room for a Different Response

I'm learning that I don't have to respond to every situation immediately.

Sometimes I can listen without offering advice.

Sometimes I can acknowledge a concern without taking ownership of the outcome.

And sometimes the most helpful thing I can do is recognize that I don't have an answer.

For someone who has spent a career helping people navigate challenges, that isn't always easy.

But there's something valuable about recognizing the difference between being helpful and feeling responsible.

At ThriveWell, our **Arrive • Notice • Release • Return™** approach reminds us that we can pause, recognize what we're experiencing, make room for restoration, and move forward with greater awareness.

For me, that includes paying attention to where I'm putting my energy and whether it's helping me live in a way that reflects what matters most.

## One Thought for the Week Ahead

**What would change if I focused less on what I want someone else to do and more on how I want to show up in my own life?**

Maybe the answer isn't about doing more.

Maybe it's about recognizing that some things were never ours to fix in the first place.

And perhaps making room for ourselves begins with understanding that we can care deeply without carrying everything.
