I've always been someone who likes to have a plan.

In my professional life, planning, preparing for change, and thinking through what might happen next have been important parts of my work.

And in my personal life, I've often approached challenges in much the same way.

If something feels uncertain, my instinct is to gather information, consider the possibilities, and figure out what I should do.

Sometimes that helps.

But I've also learned that not everything can be planned, predicted, or worked through on my preferred timeline.

And that's not always easy to accept.

## When I Don't Have the Answers

I think uncertainty can be especially uncomfortable when other people depend on us.

We want to know that our families will be okay, that our work will go well, that someone we care about will make a good decision, or that a difficult situation will improve.

When we don't know what comes next, it's tempting to fill in the gaps ourselves.

We start imagining what could go wrong, replaying past experiences, or preparing for problems that haven't happened.

I've noticed how much energy that can take.

And I've been learning that constantly searching for certainty doesn't necessarily make me feel more secure.

Sometimes it simply leaves less room for anything else.

## What Hope Means to Me

I've been thinking about hope differently.

I don't think hope requires us to believe that everything will turn out exactly as we want.

It doesn't mean ignoring problems, pretending we're not worried, or expecting other people to change.

For me, hope is becoming more about allowing for the possibility that something meaningful or good can still happen, even when I don't know what that will look like.

Maybe a situation will improve.

Maybe I'll discover a resource I hadn't considered.

Maybe I'll find a different way to respond.

Or perhaps I'll learn that I can manage something difficult without having every answer in advance.

That possibility matters.

## Making Space for a Different Perspective

When I'm worried about something, I've started trying to notice the stories I'm telling myself.

Am I responding to what's happening, or to what I'm afraid might happen?

Am I assuming that because something was difficult before, it will always be difficult?

Have I left room for a different outcome?

These questions don't make uncertainty disappear. They help me recognize that my first interpretation isn't necessarily the only one.

And sometimes that makes room for a little more perspective.

It can also help to talk with someone I trust, take a practical step where I can, or give a situation time to unfold without repeatedly trying to manage it.

## A Few Questions for Your Journal

If you're carrying some uncertainty into this holiday season, consider reflecting on these questions:

1.  What situation in my life feels uncertain right now?

2.  What am I assuming about how it will turn out?

3.  Is there another possibility I haven't allowed myself to consider?

4.  Who or what helps me feel supported when I don't have the answers?

5.  What is one practical step I can take while allowing the rest to unfold?

You might also finish this sentence:

*Even though I don't know what comes next, I'm willing to make room for \_\_\_\_\_\_.*

## A Little More Room for Hope

As the year draws to a close, we may find ourselves thinking about what we expected this year to be and what actually happened.

Perhaps some things turned out better than we imagined. Others may still feel unresolved.

I'm learning that I can acknowledge both.

I don't have to minimize what's difficult to recognize what's possible.

At ThriveWell, our Arrive • Notice • Release • Return™ approach encourages us to recognize what we're experiencing without immediately judging it or trying to change everything.

For me, that sometimes means giving myself permission not to know.

**One thought to carry into your week:**

*What possibility am I willing to leave room for?*

Maybe hope begins with accepting that the future hasn't been written yet.
